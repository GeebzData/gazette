AI computing power becomes a traded commodity

The Trends

  1. 14 showsAI is squeezing traditional software profits and changing how engineers get paidWhat's the story
    • Christina Cacioppo, CEO of security software firm Vanta, said on Invest Like the Best that corporate buyers immediately reject extra per-use fees for AI features. Software companies must absorb those computing costs into flat subscription prices, which shrinks their profit margins unless the tools keep customers from canceling.
    • On Empire, venture investor Michael Dempsey of Compound warned that open-source AI programs now copy standard business software on demand, while AI model usage grows tenfold a year. He now puts 70% of his fund into physical science and biotech startups because software tools without proprietary data or workflows will lose their pricing power.
    • Alex Lieberman and CJ Hess of AI engineering firm Tenex told Latent Space that 90% of big companies give staff AI chatbots, but only 5% run automated AI workers outside engineering. Tenex pays its coders for completed work instead of hours, creating a 4x pay gap between its fastest and slowest developers.
    What's next
    • Gross profit margins at public software companies: if margins dip while subscription prices stay flat, vendors are eating rising AI computing bills without gaining pricing power.
    • Enterprise software renewal rates: if companies start replacing single-purpose apps with general AI assistants like Town's email tool, mid-sized software vendors will see customer cancellations spike.
    On: Invest Like the Best  ·  Empire  ·  a16z Podcast  ·  Latent Space
  2. 23 showsCrypto money shifts from startups to tradable tokens, and traditional finance pushes backWhat's the story
    • Arthur Cheong of DeFiance Capital said on Forward Guidance that funds trading liquid tokens (crypto assets anyone can buy or sell on an exchange) returned 20% to 100% this year while Bitcoin lagged. Because struggling tokens drop 50% every six months, traders profit by betting against losers and backing winners like Hyperliquid.
    • On Empire, venture investor Michael Dempsey said crypto startup funding has gone dormant, pushing money toward discounted shares in established firms like Chainalysis and tokens that collect real user fees. For new crypto startups, that means fewer investors willing to back an idea before it has users.
    • The rise of offshore trading venues is drawing fire from traditional finance: CME Group CEO Terry Duffy argued on Odd Lots that US regulators must bar venues like Hyperliquid unless they verify users' identities. Offering 250x borrowed money without that check, he said, sets off forced-selling spirals and breaks US trading rules.
    What's next
    • CFTC enforcement actions against perpetual futures (crypto contracts with no expiration date): reclassifying them as swaps would block US traders from offshore venues and hand volume back to CME.
    • Secondary market prices for locked venture tokens: if three-month boom-and-bust cycles continue, venture funds holding multi-year lockups will have to mark down their portfolios.
    On: Odd Lots  ·  Empire  ·  Forward Guidance
  3. 32 showsTraders are turning AI computing hours into a financial commodity like oilWhat's the story
    • On Odd Lots, DRW trading firm founder Don Wilson said AI cloud provider CoreWeave must offer bond buyers a 15% return because it rents chips to unrated customers. That slows data center building, so Wilson is creating futures contracts, pending US approval, that let cloud providers lock in future revenue and borrow more cheaply.
    • Evan Conrad, CEO of chip-time marketplace SF Compute, told Latent Space that traders rent Nvidia B300 chips for $5 to $6 an hour and resell that power running AI models for $25 to $40. Customers on his exchange also resell unused hours to recover $25 of every $100 spent, which lowers software builders' computing bills.
    • DRW is designing a contract, Wilson said on Odd Lots, that tracks the gap between raw chip rental costs and what AI companies charge per word generated. Oil refiners use a similar contract to lock in the price gap between crude oil and gasoline, so AI firms could protect themselves from sudden price drops.
    What's next
    • The Commodity Futures Trading Commission's post-election ruling on GPU futures: approval lets Wall Street banks finance AI data centers at lower interest rates, speeding up construction.
    • Resale prices for older Nvidia chips on SF Compute: if buyers only trade the newest hardware, cloud providers holding three-to-five-year leases on older servers could face heavy losses.
    On: Latent Space  ·  Odd Lots

Five things worth knowing
  1. 1Internal intermediate calculations prove models plan multi-step outputs in advance; Claude selects target rhyme words in its hidden layers before generating the preceding lines.

  5. 5Caveat: Inspecting internal numbers cannot catch every failure mode because researchers understand only a fraction of the model's intermediate states, so deceptive behaviors can still remain hidden.

Guests  ·  Alex Pruden Co-founder and CEO of Project Eleven  ·  Conor Deegan Co-founder and CTO of Project Eleven

Alex Pruden and Conor Deegan of Project Eleven, pitching their new Strongpoint custody product, warn that AI-assisted math solvers threaten elliptic curve cryptography faster than quantum hardware. Blockchains must design crypto-agile architectures supporting multiple signature schemes simultaneously, rather than retreating into rigid hash-based bunker modes or betting on single NIST standards.

Five things worth knowing
  1. 1Frontier AI models are breaking structured post-quantum schemes like Hawk and McEliece today, proving that algorithmic math problems fall to machine intelligence well before quantum hardware matures.
  2. 2Hash-based digital signatures avoid vulnerable algebraic structures entirely, but their larger payload sizes and slower verification impose severe bandwidth and throughput penalties on global blockchains.
  3. 3Privacy-preserving protocols like Zcash face existential exposure if public-key encryption fails; while signature schemes can rely on hashes, interactive zero-knowledge proofs and shared secrets require rich mathematical trapdoors.
  4. 4Social rollbacks and pre-deployed validator kill switches cannot replace cryptographic security, as coordinate-level resets destroy permissionless finality across decentralized networks.
  5. 5Caveat: Hash functions have historically suffered far more practical breaks and deprecations than elliptic curves, making hasty retreats to hash-only primitives an unproven risk hedge.
“we need to assume anything could break.”Unchained  ·  53:27
