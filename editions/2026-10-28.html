Corporate buyers balk at frontier AI costs

In brief
  1. 1Investors warn that the AI data center boom rests on fragile startup spending Tech giants are pouring hundreds of billions into computing centers, but most of that money comes from venture-backed startups rather than ordinary business customers.
  2. 2Corporate buyers expect cheap open-weight AI models to take most routine work Companies are moving routine office work off expensive flagship AI and onto free-to-download models run on their own servers, at a fraction of the price.
  3. 3Venture investors warn that AI coding tools will crush per-seat software margins Automated coding tools let businesses build custom software in-house, forcing standard software firms to slash prices as clients cancel per-worker monthly subscriptions.
  4. 4AI shopping bots steer wealthy buyers to pricier goods while retailers block checkouts Automated shopping apps promise to hunt for deals, but tests show they read user emails to gauge personal wealth and pick pricier items for wealthier shoppers.
  5. 5Automated AI hacks push Washington to license private cyber strikes on scam cartels Overseas scam rings now use automated tools to break into computer networks, and the White House is answering by authorizing private contractors to hack them back.

Once spare capacity on the 900-gigawatt grid vanishes, utilities gain pricing power while cloud operators face hard growth caps.
    • Pushing back on bubble fears, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told the a16z Podcast that his $220 billion capital budget this year leaves customer demand unmet, with cloud capacity nearly booked through 2027.   2. 24 showsCorporate buyers expect cheap open-weight AI models to take most routine workBusinesses initially paid steep fees to run every task through flagship closed AI models from OpenAI and Anthropic. Now companies are switching routine work to open-weight models—free-to-download AI software whose internal code can be inspected and run on private servers for $1 to $2 per million tokens (word fragments).Why it mattersIf basic machine intelligence becomes a cheap commodity like internet bandwidth, frontier AI labs would struggle to justify massive valuations. Over the next few years, the savings would flow instead to everyday businesses that cut software bills while keeping sensitive records inside their own private servers. Technology chiefs must decide whether to lock into one flagship AI vendor or build internal testing systems that swap cheap models on demand.What's the story
    • On All-In, IBM CEO Arvind Krishna and Palo Alto Networks CEO Nikesh Arora, who sell enterprise infrastructure, said open-weight models cost $1 to $2 per million tokens versus $20 to $50 for closed rivals. Requiring inspectable reasoning logs, regulated firms will shift 75% to 90% of routine tasks away from costly frontier model providers.
    • On 20VC, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella, who sells cloud tools, cited Unilever moving trade-claim tasks from a frontier model to a cheaper internal one using private benchmarks. On Invest Like the Best, investor Cliff Sosin warned token price cuts could shrink lab revenues like fiber carrier Cogent Communications, which handles 40% of internet traffic.
    • Cheap open hosting carries traps: on Y Combinator, Ollama chief Jeffrey Morgan, who runs a routing cloud, cited Artificial Analysis data showing a 28% quality gap across hosts serving the same GPT-OSS-120B model. Independent cloud hosts modify a model's numeric precision to cut their operating costs.
    The alphaAs companies use private benchmarks to route routine queries to $1-per-million-token open models, frontier labs like OpenAI and Anthropic face margin pressure on bulk corporate workloads. Second-order winners include private data center hardware suppliers and model-auditing firms: Nikesh Arora noted one major US bank already cut AWS and Google Cloud spending to build six standalone data centers. While bulls argue cheaper tokens will expand total spending, Cliff Sosin warns revenue could shrink once coding tasks saturate over the next three years.
    • IBM▲ BenefitsIts consulting and hybrid-cloud business makes money helping regulated enterprises deploy and audit open-weight models on private servers, a shift that enterprise software earnings will track.
    • MicrosoftMSFT◆ DependsAzure loses high-margin proprietary OpenAI token fees when clients like Unilever switch to cheaper internal models, but captures cloud hosting and evaluation tooling revenue across both.
    • Palo Alto NetworksPANW▲ BenefitsRegulated companies shifting to self-hosted open-weight models and autonomous agents need external network monitoring and compliance logging, feeding core cybersecurity software demand.
    • Dell TechnologiesDELL▲ BenefitsOur analysisEnterprise server makers gain direct hardware orders when major banks pull workloads off public clouds to build standalone private data centers for open-weight models.
    What's next
    • Cloud provider disclosures of open-weight token volume versus proprietary API billings over the next three years will show whether open models take 80% of traffic while closed labs keep half the revenue.
    • Model creators releasing automated verifier suites to publicly grade third-party cloud hosts will test whether developers can trust cheap open-model endpoints without suffering a 28% accuracy drop.

    • On This Week in Startups, Alex Wilhelm noted retailers are blocking shopping bots like Mew and Instinct at checkout, while OpenAI's James Sun told the a16z Podcast that ad networks discard bot visits. Because bots ignore banner ads, merchants risk losing high-margin ad revenue if they allow bots or losing sales if they block them.
    • On Invest Like the Best, investor Cliff Sosin predicted consumer bots will switch banks and utilities for a 50-cent monthly saving, ending customer inertia and forcing service firms to price near cost. Meanwhile, Instacart said its price gaps were randomized tests, and Odd Lots host Joe Weisenthal noted stranded buyers with 5% phone battery gladly pay more.
    The alphaGrocers and online marketplaces rely heavily on sponsored ads and shopper data for profit—Lindsay Owens noted that Kroger generates 30% of its net operating income from its division selling customer transaction data. If autonomous shopping bots bypass store apps, that high-margin retail ad revenue shrinks even if merchandise volume holds steady. Second-order losers under Cliff Sosin's forecast would be consumer banks and insurers that profit from customer inertia, though regulators would first have to bar bots from taking seller kickbacks.

    • KrogerKR▼ At riskWith 30% of its net operating income coming from its data and advertising division, its profit margins face pressure if agentic checkout tools strip away retail ad views.
    • ShopifySHOP▲ BenefitsOur analysisUnlike ad-dependent marketplaces, its merchant checkout software earns fees on total payment volume, meaning it benefits if independent merchants open checkouts to capture sales from AI shopping bots.

    • Ad-tech platforms updating traffic-auditing standards for automated software agents will show whether publishers and retailers can charge advertisers for bot visits or will keep blocking agents at checkout.

To acquire proven automated defenses, a16z crypto's Noah Levine noted on Tokenized that S&P agreed to buy auditor OpenZeppelin and OpenAI held talks for Trail of Bits.
    • Offensive licensing faces limits: on Unchained, TRM Labs' Ari Redbord noted strict Justice Department vetting will restrict how many firms can legally hack back, while Chris Giancarlo warned lines blur when cartels have tacit state backing.     The alphaTech and financial giants are buying crypto security auditors like OpenZeppelin and Trail of Bits because irreversible blockchain thefts forced those shops to build automated formal verification (math-based code proofs) years ago. Inside corporations, security budgets are also shifting to agent containment—Microsoft built Agent 365 and Anthropic built external credential vaults so AI agents never touch raw passwords. While cyber contractors eye bounty revenue from seizing cartel assets, strict Justice Department sign-offs and murky state ties could keep offensive mandates narrow.

    • Booz Allen HamiltonBAH▲ BenefitsOur analysisAs a top federal cyber contractor, its defense and civil cyber practices stand to win authorized offensive mandates once Justice Department rules take effect late this year.

    • Formal rulemaking from the Department of Justice scheduled late this year under the August White House directive will show whether private cybersecurity firms receive broad recovery bounties or narrow case-by-case warrants.
    • The closing of OpenAI's reported deal to buy crypto security firm Trail of Bits will confirm whether frontier AI labs are bringing automated code-auditing tools in-house.
