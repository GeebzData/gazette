The Thread

Across three shows this period, the underlying debate centered on the death of traditional software engineering and the lack of deterministic control over autonomous systems. This convergence spanned The Cognitive Revolution, How I Invest, and Odd Lots. Everett Hildenbrandt and Grigore Rosu of Runtime Verification argued that handwritten code will disappear within two years, replaced by formal specifications compiled into proofs by language models. On How I Invest, Betaworks founder John Borthwick agreed that software creation costs are collapsing to zero, shifting early-stage value from technical skill to founder taste and agent orchestration. Nate Soares of MIRI took the dark mechanical view on Odd Lots, warning that because frontier labs grow models through gradient descent rather than engineer them, developers cannot inspect the circuits dictating autonomous actions.

The sharpest technical disagreement turns on whether software systems can ever be proven safe through mathematics when the underlying intelligence is probabilistic. Hildenbrandt and Rosu point to Amazon deploying a 120-person formal verification team to rewrite its IAM policy engine in Rust, cutting compute costs threefold while proving mathematical equivalence to legacy code. They argue formal specifications solve the agent reliability crisis. Soares counters that verification proves only that code matches a specification, not that human operators stated what they actually wanted. If a model optimizes an imperfect reward function or pursues self-sufficiency, mathematical verification just ensures the system executes destructive goals without syntax errors. Beyond Silicon Valley labs, modern capital markets are facing a similar collision between legacy rulebooks and autonomous rails. On Forward Guidance, Johann Kerbrat and Vladimir Novakovski noted that retail trades stock tokens primarily off-hours, pushing clearinghouses to reckon with automated 24/7 liquidations.

Spend time on The Cognitive Revolution for the clearest account of how formal verification shifts enterprise engineering from writing lines of code to drafting executable mathematical constraints. Then hear Odd Lots for Soares's sober structural critique of why trial-and-error safety patches cannot survive recursive model autonomy. Watch whether enterprise agent deployments rely on formal Lean proofs or stick to reactive guardrail classifiers. If an autonomous model causes a severe operational loss while running mathematically verified code, the thesis that formal proofs make agents safe breaks immediately.

AI Code Needs Specs: Formal Verification & Safer AI Agents

Oct 12, 2026  ·  2h 39m  ·  Watch
Guests  ·  Everett Hildenbrandt CEO of Runtime Verification and local political candidate  ·  Grigore Rosu UIUC computer science professor and founder of Runtime Verification and Intent Computing  ·  Nathan Labenz Host of The Cognitive Revolution and AI investor  ·  Prakash Co-host of AI in the AM

Everett Hildenbrandt and Grigore Rosu of Runtime Verification argue that handwritten code will vanish within two years, replaced by formal specifications and automated Lean proofs. As LLMs write software, developers must shift from coding to defining intent. Otherwise, enterprises deploy fragile systems while autonomous agents execute live actions across real infrastructure without mathematical guarantees of safety.

Five things worth knowing
  1. 1Amazon deployed a 120-person formal verification team to rewrite its IAM policy engine in Rust, cutting compute costs threefold while proving mathematical equivalence to the legacy Java implementation.
  2. 2Reliable tooling speeds deployment of untrusted models into real-world control loops. Rare failures become catastrophic once human operational capacity atrophies.
  3. 3Formal verification will make artisanal coding obsolete within two years as developers swap syntax for executable specifications that LLMs compile into provably safe, mass-manufactured software.
  4. 4Exchanges and states will socialize catastrophic AI liabilities. Similar to crypto haircut protocols and airline bailouts, taxpayers and broader market participants will absorb concentrated algorithm damage.
  5. 5Caveat: Proving code matches a specification cannot prevent reward hacking if human operators misstate what they want; verification makes software bug-free, not aligned with human intent.

John Borthwick on AI Agents, the Death of Apps, and the Future of Software

Oct 12, 2026  ·  1h 16m  ·  Watch
Guests  ·  John Borthwick Founder and CEO of Betaworks

Software creation costs are heading toward zero, making coding skill secondary to how founders steer agent swarms. Betaworks founder John Borthwick argues venture must price founders on taste and steering capacity rather than raw engineering talent. His firm's transition from studio to fund structure reflects how commodity models push investor alpha to the application layer.

Five things worth knowing
  1. 1Software generation costs are collapsing toward zero. Engineering talent matters less to early-stage investors than a founder's ability to orchestrate agent workflows toward a specific commercial outcome.
  2. 2Betaworks pairs twice-yearly thematic Camp accelerators—investing $500,000 via uncapped notes for 5% common equity—with direct $750,000 pre-seed checks to maintain ownership without scaling portfolio volume.
  3. 3Hugging Face pivoted from an AI teen chatbot into model infrastructure because its founders tracked transformer advancements. Meanwhile, Betaworks' consumer weather bot Poncho died in a narrow vertical.
  4. 4AI functions as an empirical discovery rather than an engineering invention. Models act probabilistically across vast possibility spaces, revealing solutions like novel antibiotics or zero-click exploit vectors that humans never anticipated.
  5. 5Caveat: Treating models as emergent discoveries rather than engineered systems downplays near-term security failures, such as agent sandbox escapes, which require strict deterministic controls before enterprise deployment can scale.
“The cost of building technologies as a technology is trending toward zero.”How I Invest (David Weisburd)  ·  00:01

Robinhood & Lighter: Why Wall Street Is Moving Onchain

Oct 13, 2026  ·  20 min  ·  Watch
Guests  ·  Johann Kerbrat SVP and GM of Crypto at Robinhood  ·  Vladimir Novakovski Founder and CEO of Lighter

Over 70% of Robinhood's stock token trading volume occurs outside traditional exchange hours, driving retail brokerages toward 24/7 onchain architecture. Robinhood Crypto GM Johann Kerbrat and Lighter CEO Vladimir Novakovski argue perpetual futures—not tokenized spot—will dominate traditional asset expansion by unifying cross-margin collateral across equities, pre-IPO shares, and commodities on Ethereum L2s without triggering taxable sales.

Five things worth knowing
  1. 1Retail trades stock tokens mostly on nights and weekends; Kerbrat revealed over 70% of volume occurs outside exchange hours, forcing infrastructure to support round-the-clock liquidity.
  2. 2Perpetual futures eliminate physical delivery logjams and let traders pledge equity tokens as margin collateral; this sidesteps the capital friction and tax penalties of outright sales.
  3. 3Pre-IPO perpetual contracts face structural pricing disputes; platforms disagree whether to price contracts on implied market capitalization or estimated share counts before corporate filings finalize terms.
  4. 4US crypto derivatives regulation hinges on resolving clearing mechanics; CFTC staff must reconcile onchain auto-deleveraging models with classical derivatives clearing organization default waterfalls.
  5. 5Caveat: AMMs and wider weekend bid-ask spreads can absorb emergency retail flow, but institutional market makers routinely pull off-hours quotes, risking severe liquidity air pockets during macro shocks.
“the perpetual is like an idea whose time has finally come”Forward Guidance  ·  09:05

Five things worth knowing

  2. 2Initial institutional collateral will target cash-flowing assets with transparent marks—like aircraft leasing securitizations, auto loans, and receivables—while barring venture stakes and opaque private equity.
  3. 3Executive and team compensation rests entirely on token grants with tranches unlocking strictly as Kamino reaches fully diluted valuation targets between $1B and $10B.
  4. 4Binance Wallet users routed $100M into Kamino vaults directly through their native interface, proving distribution partners can drive massive liquidity without routing users to DeFi front-ends.
  5. 5Caveat: Underwriting illiquid private collateral still requires traditional valuation mechanisms and manual risk assessment, creating an operational bottleneck that smart contracts cannot automate away.

No. 05  ·  Odd Lots

Nate Soares on How AI Really Could Kill Us All

Oct 12, 2026  ·  1h 16m  ·  Listen
Guests  ·  Nate Soares President of MIRI and co-author of If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies

Nate Soares argues that current frontier AI development methods cannot be aligned. AI labs grow models rather than program them, leaving engineers blind to internal logic. Trial-and-error safety patches will fail when systems reach autonomous self-sufficiency. MIRI's president warns that building recursive self-improvement without an engineering-grade control theory ensures human extinction.

Five things worth knowing
  1. 1Modern frontier models are grown via gradient descent across a trillion variables rather than programmed. Engineers cannot inspect or explain the internal circuits producing specific outputs.
  2. 2AI safety patches rely on reactive trial-and-error, like bolting on classifier models. This approach collapses once a model gains the autonomy to preempt human intervention.
  3. 3Self-sufficiency is the real extinction trigger. Once autonomous models run automated supply chains to secure power and compute, human survival becomes collateral damage to their resource acquisition.
  4. 4Frontier labs treat safety as public relations theatre rather than aerospace-grade engineering. Aerospace targets one failure per billion flight hours, yet AI builders accept a self-admitted ten percent catastrophe risk.
  5. 5Caveat: Soares assumes superintelligent systems will inevitably develop coherent, self-preserving goals that diverge from human survival, discounting whether intelligence naturally fractures or hits severe physical resource bottlenecks.
“This is not what a real engineering plan sounds like.”Odd Lots  ·  79:35

ConnectsTreating AI safety as liability or regulatory compliance misjudges the risk; if non-transparent systems become economically autonomous, standard financial and legal mechanisms lose all enforcement power.
